How to Choose an Automated Gate System

An automated gate should match the entrance, traffic pattern and security needs of the property. Choosing a system only by price or motor rating can lead to poor performance, avoidable repairs or safety risks.

1. Start with the gate and the site

Before comparing motors, inspect the gate itself. A sliding gate needs suitable side space for the gate to travel; a swing gate needs enough clear space for its leaves to open without striking vehicles, people, walls or parked objects. Slopes, drainage, wind exposure, ground condition and the state of the hinges, rollers and tracks all affect the design.

Automation does not correct a badly aligned or mechanically damaged gate. The gate should move smoothly by hand, where its design permits, before an installer sizes and fits the automation equipment.

2. Match the system to daily use

Estimate how often the gate will open and close during a normal day, including busy periods. A private driveway, an estate entrance and a commercial facility may have very different duty cycles. Equipment should be selected for the expected frequency of use, the gate's dimensions and weight, and the manufacturer's stated limits—not simply because a motor is advertised as powerful.

  • Sliding systems: Consider track condition, side clearance, rollers, rack alignment and protection from debris.
  • Swing systems: Check hinge condition, opening angle, wind exposure and clearance for each leaf.
  • Shared entrances: Consider queue length, pedestrian movement, emergency access and how authorised users will enter during busy periods.

3. Plan safety before convenience

Remote controls, keypads, intercoms and access cards can make entry easier, but they must be combined with a safe layout. Ask the installer how the design detects or limits contact with people, vehicles and other obstacles. Depending on the gate and site, this may involve suitable photoelectric sensors, safety edges, warning devices, guarded moving parts and clear operating procedures.

Keep the gate's movement path visible and free from storage, vegetation and other obstructions. Make sure household members, staff or security personnel understand how to stop the gate and what to do if it behaves unexpectedly. Emergency release and manual operation should be explained during handover.

Safety note: Do not bypass safety sensors or attempt electrical repairs on a powered gate unless you are qualified and have followed the manufacturer's isolation procedure. Keep children away from moving gate parts.

4. Consider power and access control

Ask what happens during a power interruption. Depending on the system, a suitable battery backup may support limited operation, while a manual release may be needed when power is unavailable. Confirm the backup's expected capacity, charging requirements and maintenance needs rather than assuming every system includes one.

Choose access methods around the people who will use the entrance. Remote controls may suit a residence; a keypad, intercom or managed access credential may be more appropriate for a workplace or shared property. Plan how lost remotes or staff departures will be handled, and avoid sharing access codes unnecessarily.

5. Compare the full cost, not just the motor

A quotation should make the scope understandable. Ask whether it includes site preparation, brackets, rack or arm hardware, cabling, safety devices, access controls, commissioning, user instruction and any civil or electrical work. Clarify exclusions, warranty terms, response arrangements and the cost of routine servicing.

Cheap equipment can become expensive if it is unsuitable for the gate or difficult to service. Conversely, buying a larger system than the site needs may waste money. Compare offers on the same scope and request the manufacturer's model and rated operating limits.

6. Plan maintenance from day one

Maintenance depends on the gate type and manufacturer's instructions. Keep tracks and travel paths clear; inspect visible fixings, hinges, rollers and signs of corrosion; listen for unusual noise; and test the safety features at the recommended intervals. Do not apply grease or lubricant indiscriminately—some components require specific products or should remain dry.

Record faults and repairs. If the gate becomes unusually slow, jerks, reverses unexpectedly, makes new noises or fails a safety test, stop using automatic operation if it is safe to do so and arrange a competent inspection.

Questions to answer before requesting a quotation

  • What are the gate type, approximate dimensions and condition?
  • How many opening cycles are expected on an ordinary and a busy day?
  • What safety devices and emergency-release arrangements are proposed?
  • Is backup power required, and what operation will it support?
  • Which users need access, and how will access credentials be managed?
  • What is included in installation, handover, warranty and maintenance?

A site assessment is the best way to turn these questions into a suitable specification. NGP Integrated Services Limited can discuss your requirements and help define the scope of a smart-security or automated-gate project.
