A Practical ICT Maintenance Checklist

Routine ICT maintenance reduces avoidable interruptions and helps a business understand what equipment it relies on. A simple documented routine is often more useful than waiting until a device or network fails.

Prepared by NGP Integrated Services Limited · Updated 26 October 2026 · General educational guidance

1. Keep an accurate equipment and service inventory

Maintain a current list of computers, printers, network equipment, access points, business phones, software subscriptions and important online services. Record the device owner or location, business purpose, operating system, warranty or support information, and the person responsible for maintenance. Do not store passwords in an open spreadsheet.

An inventory helps the business identify unsupported devices, renew subscriptions on time, plan replacements and know which equipment needs attention after a fault. Update it whenever equipment is purchased, moved, repaired or retired.

2. Manage updates deliberately

Operating-system, browser, application and network-device updates can fix security problems and reliability defects. Enable supported automatic updates where appropriate, and establish a process for devices that need testing or scheduled maintenance. Avoid leaving critical systems unsupported indefinitely.

Before a major update, confirm that important files are backed up and that essential applications or peripherals are compatible. Restart devices when required, check that business-critical functions still work and record any failed update. Do not download installers from unknown websites or use unlicensed software.

3. Test backups instead of assuming they work

Identify which information the business cannot afford to lose: finance records, customer files, project documents, email, shared folders and configuration data. Decide how often each type needs backing up and who checks the results. For important data, consider a backup copy separated from everyday user accounts and the main network so that one compromised account cannot easily destroy every copy.

A successful backup job is not proof that the business can recover. Periodically restore a sample file or test the documented recovery process. Protect backup accounts with strong authentication and limit who can delete backup copies.

4. Review accounts and permissions

Give staff only the access needed for their roles. Use individual accounts rather than sharing one administrator login across a team. Enable multi-factor authentication where available, especially for email, cloud storage, remote access and administrator accounts. Remove or disable accounts promptly when people leave or change responsibilities.

Review password-reset and account-recovery options, and make sure more than one authorised person can recover a critical business account if the usual administrator is unavailable. Never send passwords or verification codes to someone who contacts you unexpectedly.

5. Check the network and power environment

Record the normal network layout, internet service contact, router and access-point locations, and the names of key devices. When users report slow connections, check whether the problem affects one device, one area or the whole site before replacing equipment. Note recurring dropouts and the time they occur so that a service provider or technician has useful evidence.

Keep networking equipment ventilated and protected from accidental damage. Check cables and connectors for visible damage without pulling live equipment apart. Use suitable surge protection and backup power where appropriate, and arrange electrical work through qualified personnel.

6. Protect endpoints and business information

  • Use supported operating systems and reputable, up-to-date security protection.
  • Lock screens when unattended and securely erase devices before disposal or reassignment.
  • Train staff to verify unexpected payment changes, attachments and login prompts through a trusted channel.
  • Restrict removable storage and software installation where the business risk requires it.
  • Keep a simple way for staff to report lost devices, suspicious messages or unusual account activity.

7. Document faults and recovery steps

For each incident, record the date, affected service, symptoms, business impact, action taken and whether the issue returned. This helps distinguish isolated faults from recurring problems. Keep a short recovery sheet for critical services: who to contact, where backups are stored, which systems are most important and how the business can continue temporarily.

Do not write sensitive passwords in the recovery sheet. Store technical documentation in a controlled location and make sure an authorised backup contact can access it when necessary.

A simple maintenance rhythm

  • Weekly: Review backup alerts, reported faults and unusual account notifications.
  • Monthly: Check update status, inventory changes, network performance and a sample restore.
  • Quarterly: Review user access, recovery contacts, warranties and recurring incidents.
  • After an incident: Record what happened, restore safely, and update the checklist to prevent a repeat.
Important: This checklist is general guidance, not a substitute for a security assessment or a backup and recovery plan designed for the organisation's systems and legal obligations.
